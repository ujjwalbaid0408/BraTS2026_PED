\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Pediatric brain tumours are rare, highly heterogeneous, and biologically distinct
from the adult gliomas on which most segmentation models are trained, which makes
their automated delineation in multi-parametric MRI (mpMRI) uniquely challenging.
We present our submission to Task~2 of the MICCAI BraTS-PED 2026 challenge, which
asks for the voxel-wise segmentation of four tumour sub-regions ---
enhancing tumour (ET), non-enhancing tumour (NET), cystic component (CC) and
peritumoral edema (ED) --- from four co-registered MRI sequences (T1, T1-Gd, T2,
T2-FLAIR). Building on the winning recipe of the BraTS-METS~2025 and BraTS-PED~2025
challenges, our pipeline (i)~\emph{skull-strips} every case, because the
challenge images retain skull and neck tissue that confounds learning;
(ii)~trains a self-configuring \textbf{nnU-Net~v2} with a \textbf{residual-encoder
(ResEnc-L)} 3D full-resolution U-Net as a five-fold cross-validation ensemble; and
(iii)~applies \emph{lesion-wise} post-processing that relabels or removes tiny,
spurious connected components, directly targeting the challenge's lesion-wise
Dice / Hausdorff metric. In five-fold cross-validation over the training cohort
($n\!\approx\!285$) the method attains a mean lesion-wise Dice of
\result{WT}{0.834} (whole tumour, WT) and \result{TC}{0.797} (tumour core, TC); on a
held-out internal test set of ten unseen cases it attains voxel-wise Dice of
\result{WT}{0.914} (WT) and \result{TC}{0.876} (TC). We package the full method as a
reproducible, GPU-ready Docker/Apptainer container conforming to the
BraTS-Lighthouse submission contract. All code, training scripts and this report are
released openly.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} pediatric brain tumour, segmentation, nnU-Net,
skull-stripping, lesion-wise metric, mpMRI, BraTS, deep learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Central nervous system (CNS) tumours are the most common solid tumours of childhood
and the leading cause of cancer-related death in children. Although individually
rare --- pediatric tumours account for roughly $3.6\%$ of all brain tumours ---
pediatric high-grade gliomas (pHGG), including diffuse midline glioma (DMG), carry a
dismal prognosis, with five-year survival below $30\%$ for high-grade disease and a
median survival of only $\sim\!11$ months for DMG. Accurate, reproducible
delineation of the tumour and its sub-compartments on MRI underpins diagnosis,
surgical and radiotherapy planning, treatment-response assessment (per the RAPNO
criteria) and longitudinal monitoring of these children.

In current practice, tumour extent is most often summarised by one- or
two-dimensional diameter measurements on a single slice, because full volumetric
contouring by hand is prohibitively time-consuming --- tens of minutes per study, per
timepoint, per reader. Yet longitudinal volumetry is far more sensitive to subtle
growth or treatment response than linear measures, and the RAPNO working groups have
explicitly called for reliable volumetric tools in pediatric neuro-oncology trials.
Automated, reproducible segmentation is the enabling technology: it converts a
many-minute manual task into a sub-minute automated one and removes the reader as a
source of measurement noise, which is precisely what multi-centre trials of rare
pediatric tumours need to reach statistical power with limited patient numbers.

Manual segmentation by expert neuroradiologists is slow, costly and subject to
substantial inter- and intra-rater variability, particularly for the diffuse,
ill-defined margins typical of pediatric tumours. Deep learning has driven automated
adult brain-tumour segmentation to near-expert accuracy, but pediatric tumours
present a \emph{domain shift} that models trained on adult cohorts do not capture:
the subtle, frequently non-enhancing appearance of many DMGs differs markedly from
the ring-enhancing necrosis typical of adult glioblastoma, and the sub-region
definitions themselves (a distinct cystic component, a non-enhancing tumour core
that often dominates) differ from the adult BraTS labels. Dedicated pediatric models
are therefore essential, and the BraTS-PED challenge series provides the largest
expert-annotated pediatric cohort to date for developing and benchmarking them.

A second, often under-appreciated, source of difficulty is purely technical: the
BraTS-PED images are released \emph{with} the skull and surrounding soft tissue
intact, unlike the historically skull-stripped adult BraTS data. The skull is a
large, high-contrast structure entirely irrelevant to the tumour, and both top-ranked
2025 teams identified it as a primary confounder that must be removed before
learning. A third difficulty is the \emph{small} size of the cohort (a few hundred
cases) combined with severe class imbalance: the enhancing tumour and cystic
component appear in only a minority of cases, so a model can easily hallucinate small
false-positive components that the challenge's lesion-wise metric punishes harshly.

The BraTS-PED track itself has matured rapidly. Introduced in 2023 with a few dozen
annotated subjects, it has grown each year in both cohort size and annotation
consistency, and the 2024/2025 editions standardised the four-label scheme (ET, NET,
CC, ED) and the lesion-wise evaluation adopted here. Each edition has confirmed the
same empirical pattern: the winning systems are not architecturally exotic but are
built on disciplined pre-processing (skull-stripping above all), strong
self-configuring backbones, ensembling, and metric-aware post-processing. Our system
is a deliberate, reproducible distillation of that pattern, instrumented so that every
stage can be inspected, re-run, and re-used.

This report describes our Task-2 system, which is engineered around these three
difficulties. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A \textbf{skull-stripping front-end} that removes non-brain tissue before
        learning, applied \emph{identically} at training and inference time so the
        network never experiences a train/test domain gap.
  \item A \textbf{residual-encoder nnU-Net~v2} (ResEnc-L, 3D full-resolution),
        trained as a \textbf{five-fold cross-validation ensemble} to extract maximum
        value from a small dataset.
  \item \textbf{Lesion-wise post-processing} tuned to the challenge metric, which
        relabels tiny ET/CC components into NET and removes tiny ED components.
  \item A fully \textbf{reproducible, containerised} pipeline (Docker + Apptainer)
        with automatic, checkpoint-resumable SLURM training, validated on a held-out
        internal test set and released openly with all code and scripts.
\end{itemize}
The remainder of the report details the task (\S\ref{sec:task}), surveys the relevant
literature (\S\ref{sec:lit}), describes the dataset (\S\ref{sec:data}), our proposed
approach (\S\ref{sec:approach}) and network architecture (\S\ref{sec:arch}), the
implementation and hardware (\S\ref{sec:impl}), and presents quantitative and
qualitative results (\S\ref{sec:results}), followed by discussion
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}), limitations (\S\ref{sec:limits}) and conclusions
(\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\section{The Task}
\label{sec:task}
\textbf{BraTS-PED 2026, Task 2} is the automated segmentation of pre-treatment
pediatric brain tumours from mpMRI. Each subject provides four co-registered,
$1\,\text{mm}^3$ isotropic, $240\times240\times155$ volumes, all rigidly aligned to a
common anatomical template and resampled to the same grid:
native T1 (T1N), post-contrast T1 (T1C / T1-Gd), T2-weighted (T2W) and
T2-FLAIR (T2F). The four sequences are complementary: T1C highlights
blood-brain-barrier breakdown (enhancement); T2W/FLAIR delineate edema and
non-enhancing tumour; the T1N--T1C pair isolates true enhancement from intrinsic
T1 hyperintensity.

\paragraph{MRI sequences and tumour appearance.}
Each sequence contributes distinct, partially redundant evidence, and the
sub-regions are defined by their \emph{joint} signature rather than any single
contrast. Enhancing tumour (ET) appears bright on T1C relative to T1N, reflecting
contrast leakage through a disrupted blood--brain barrier; crucially, comparing T1N
and T1C is necessary to separate genuine enhancement from intrinsically T1-bright
tissue (e.g.\ blood products, fat, proteinaceous fluid). Non-enhancing tumour (NET)
is the solid tumour that does \emph{not} enhance --- common and often dominant in
pediatric diffuse midline glioma --- and is best seen as T2/FLAIR signal abnormality
without corresponding T1C enhancement. The cystic component (CC) is fluid-filled,
following CSF-like signal (bright on T2, suppressed or dark on FLAIR, dark on T1).
Peritumoral edema (ED) is vasogenic fluid in the white matter, bright on T2/FLAIR and
surrounding the tumour core. A model must therefore integrate all four channels
coherently; this is precisely the regime in which a 3D, multi-channel U-Net excels,
and it is why we feed all four co-registered sequences as input channels rather than
processing any sequence in isolation.

The reference annotation assigns every voxel to one of five classes:
background~(0), \textbf{ET}~(1, enhancing tumour), \textbf{NET}~(2, non-enhancing
tumour), \textbf{CC}~(3, cystic component) and \textbf{ED}~(4, peritumoral edema).
Evaluation is performed on six regions: the four base labels plus two clinically
meaningful composites,
\[
\text{TC (tumour core)}=\text{ET}\cup\text{NET}\cup\text{CC},
\qquad
\text{WT (whole tumour)}=\text{ET}\cup\text{NET}\cup\text{CC}\cup\text{ED}.
\]

\paragraph{Metrics.} Submissions are ranked by the \emph{lesion-wise} Dice
Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and the \emph{lesion-wise} Hausdorff-95 distance
(HD95) / Normalized Surface Distance (NSD). The standard volumetric Dice between a
prediction $P$ and ground truth $G$ is
\[
\mathrm{DSC}(P,G)=\frac{2\,|P\cap G|}{|P|+|G|}.
\]
The \emph{lesion-wise} variant instead identifies each connected ground-truth lesion
$G_i$ (via connected-component analysis with a small-component dilation), matches it
to the overlapping prediction, computes the Dice/HD95 \emph{per lesion}, and averages
over lesions:
\[
\mathrm{LWDSC}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{DSC}(P_i,G_i),
\]
where false-positive predicted components that match no ground-truth lesion
contribute a score of zero. Two consequences are central to our design.
First, \emph{small lesions count as much as large ones}, so a model cannot ignore
the many small pediatric ET/CC foci. Second, \emph{spurious small false-positive
components are penalised severely} --- a single stray blob can drag a case's score
toward zero. This property directly motivates our post-processing
(\S\ref{sec:postproc}). For empty ground-truth regions, a correct empty prediction
scores~1 and any false positive scores~0, which makes the rare classes (ET, CC, ED)
high-variance and dominated by a handful of failure cases.

\section{Literature Survey}
\label{sec:lit}
\paragraph{The BraTS challenge lineage.}
The Multimodal Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) challenge has, since 2012,
established the de-facto benchmark for brain-tumour segmentation, standardising
mpMRI pre-processing, the ET/TC/WT region definitions, and the evaluation protocol.
The BraTS-2021 expansion to $\sim\!2000$ cases catalysed a generation of strong
volumetric segmentation models. From 2023 the challenge fragmented into a
``cluster'' of sub-challenges targeting specific clinical populations and
acquisition conditions --- adult glioma, meningioma, brain metastases (METS),
sub-Saharan Africa, and \emph{pediatrics} (PED) --- each with its own data
idiosyncrasies. The pediatric track was introduced to address the adult-to-pediatric
domain shift described above.

\paragraph{nnU-Net as the dominant backbone.}
Since its introduction by Isensee \emph{et al.}~\cite{nnunet}, the self-configuring
\textbf{nnU-Net} has been the backbone of the overwhelming majority of winning BraTS
solutions. Rather than proposing a fixed architecture, nnU-Net automatically infers
pre-processing, patch size, network topology, batch size, training schedule and
inference strategy from a ``dataset fingerprint'' (image spacings, intensity
distributions, sizes), yielding a remarkably strong, hard-to-beat baseline with no
manual tuning. The 2024/2025 BraTS results repeatedly confirmed that careful data
handling and post-processing \emph{around} nnU-Net, rather than novel architectures,
drive the leaderboard.

\paragraph{Residual-encoder U-Nets.}
The \textbf{ResEnc} presets (M/L/XL)~\cite{resenc} extend nnU-Net by adding residual
blocks to the encoder and scaling its capacity to the available GPU memory, with
re-tuned default batch and patch sizes. The accompanying study (``nnU-Net
Revisited'') demonstrated that these higher-capacity encoders, properly validated,
outperform many bespoke transformer architectures on 3D medical segmentation. The
BraTS-METS~2025 winning solution used the ResEnc-XL planner in the
\texttt{3d\_fullres} configuration, motivating our choice of the ResEnc family.

\paragraph{Transformer and hybrid architectures.}
A parallel line of work introduced attention/transformer mechanisms to volumetric
segmentation --- TransBTS, UNETR, and notably \textbf{Swin~UNETR}~\cite{swinunetr},
which uses a hierarchical shifted-window transformer encoder and won strong BraTS
placements. In pediatrics these models are typically used \emph{within an ensemble}
alongside nnU-Net rather than as standalone replacements, because the small cohort
limits the data-hungry transformer's advantage.

\paragraph{BraTS-PED 2025 top solutions.}
The rank-1 solution (Yi \emph{et al.}, ``Frequency-Aware Ensemble
Learning''~\cite{yi2025}) combined nnU-Net (with tuned initialisation), a
BraTS-2021-pretrained Swin~UNETR, and a frequency-domain HFF-Net, \emph{all on
skull-stripped data}, ensembled by probability averaging; it reported test
lesion-wise Dice of $0.729$ (ET), $0.857$ (NET), $0.627$ (CC), $0.832$ (ED),
$0.918$ (TC) and $0.926$ (WT). The rank-2 solution (Chen \&
Liang~\cite{chen2025}) showed that a \emph{single} nnU-Net~v2, trained first on
SynthStrip skull-stripped data and then fine-tuned on the original data, with
lesion-wise post-processing, achieves state-of-the-art validation performance even on
a single laptop GPU --- emphasising that \emph{methodology, not raw compute}, wins.
The rank-3 solution (PTransBTS~\cite{yu2025}) explored a hybrid
convolution--transformer architecture with learnable shape priors. Our design
distils the common, repeatedly-validated ingredients of all three systems:
\emph{skull-strip $\rightarrow$ nnU-Net $\rightarrow$ lesion-wise post-process}.

\paragraph{Skull-stripping.}
Because BraTS-PED data retain the skull, brain extraction is a recurring
pre-processing step. Classical tools (BET, ROBEX) are brittle on pediatric anatomy
and pathological contrast. \textbf{SynthStrip}~\cite{synthstrip} uses synthetic-data
training to produce robust, contrast- and age-agnostic brain masks, and is widely
adopted for pediatric and non-standard acquisitions. \textbf{HD-BET}~\cite{hdbet},
from the nnU-Net group, is a convenient, pip-installable, GPU-accelerated CNN
alternative. We support both and apply the mask consistently across all four
modalities.

\paragraph{Loss functions and class imbalance.}
Brain-tumour segmentation is severely class-imbalanced --- background dominates, and
the tumour sub-regions span three orders of magnitude in size (Table~\ref{tab:labelstats}).
The community has converged on \emph{region-based} losses, chiefly the soft Dice
loss, often combined with cross-entropy (the ``DiceCE'' loss) to retain
well-calibrated gradients where Dice saturates. Variants such as the generalized Dice,
Tversky and focal losses re-weight rare classes more aggressively, but on BraTS-scale
data the plain Dice+CE objective used by nnU-Net remains a strong, stable default,
and is what we adopt. Deep supervision --- applying the loss at multiple decoder
resolutions --- further stabilises training of deep 3D U-Nets and is standard in the
nnU-Net recipe.

\paragraph{Data augmentation and regularisation.}
With only a few hundred subjects, aggressive augmentation is essential to avoid
overfitting. The nnU-Net default ``batchgenerators'' pipeline applies random affine
transforms (rotation, scaling), elastic-free spatial deformation, Gaussian noise and
blur, brightness/contrast/gamma perturbation, simulated low resolution, and axis
mirroring, all on-the-fly. These transforms emulate realistic acquisition variability
and are a primary reason a single backbone generalises from so few cases; we rely on
them rather than introducing bespoke augmentation.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation and pre-training.}
Because the pediatric cohort is small, transferring knowledge from larger adult
datasets is attractive. Strategies seen in the BraTS-PED leaderboard include
initialising from BraTS-2021-pretrained weights (used by the rank-1 Swin~UNETR branch)
and the two-stage ``strip-then-finetune'' schedule of the rank-2 system, which first
trains on skull-stripped data and then fine-tunes on the original volumes for
robustness. Self-supervised pre-training on unlabelled MRI is a further, less-explored
direction. Our submission uses neither external pre-training nor a second stage,
leaving these as clear, well-motivated avenues for improvement (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Post-processing and ensembling.}
Connected-component post-processing --- removing or relabelling small components ---
is a standard final step in BraTS pipelines and is especially important under the
lesion-wise metric. Probability (softmax) averaging across cross-validation folds is
the canonical, low-risk ensembling strategy and is particularly effective on small
cohorts where a single fold may overfit. Both are core to our method.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:data}
We use the official BraTS-PED 2026 release: \textbf{294 training} cases (with expert
reference annotations) and \textbf{91 validation} cases (annotations withheld; used
for the public leaderboard). Each case provides the four co-registered mpMRI
sequences described in \S\ref{sec:task}, already skull-aligned and resampled to a
$240\times240\times155$, $1\,\text{mm}^3$ grid by the organisers. The annotations
were produced and approved by expert neuroradiologists following the harmonised
BraTS-PED annotation protocol.

\paragraph{Class statistics and imbalance.}
Table~\ref{tab:labelstats} summarises the strong class imbalance we measured across
the 294 training cases. NET is near-ubiquitous and large; ET is present in roughly
two thirds of cases; the cystic component and especially edema are present in only a
minority. Because the lesion-wise metric scores an empty-vs-empty region as a perfect
$1.0$ but any spurious component as $0.0$, these rare classes are simultaneously
\emph{hard to detect} and \emph{costly to over-predict}. This observation directly
shapes both our loss (the combined Dice + cross-entropy objective is robust to
imbalance) and our post-processing thresholds (\S\ref{sec:postproc}).

\paragraph{Pre-processing pipeline.}
Our pre-processing has two parts. (1)~\emph{Brain extraction}
(\texttt{skull\_strip.py}): a single brain mask is estimated and applied to all four
co-registered modalities, writing skull-stripped copies under \texttt{data\_ss/}.
HD-BET is batched in a single model load to amortise initialisation across the cohort.
(2)~\emph{nnU-Net conversion} (\texttt{prepare\_dataset.py}): the skull-stripped data
is reorganised into the nnU-Net~v2 raw layout (\texttt{imagesTr}/\texttt{labelsTr}
with the four-digit channel suffixes), 10 cases are held out deterministically, and a
\texttt{dataset.json} describing the labels and channels is generated. nnU-Net's own
planning step then computes the dataset fingerprint and applies per-channel z-score
intensity normalisation over the non-zero (brain) region, resampling to the target
spacing. Figure~\ref{fig:classdist} visualises the class statistics that drive the
loss and post-processing choices.

\paragraph{Modality-to-channel mapping.}
The four sequences are mapped to fixed nnU-Net input channels following the standard
BraTS convention: T1N$\to$\texttt{0000}, T1C$\to$\texttt{0001}, T2W$\to$\texttt{0002},
T2F$\to$\texttt{0003}.

\paragraph{Annotation protocol and label noise.}
The reference segmentations follow the harmonised BraTS-PED protocol, in which initial
automated or trainee segmentations are refined and approved by board-certified
neuroradiologists. Even so, the boundaries of pediatric tumours --- particularly the
ET/NET interface and the extent of infiltrative non-enhancing disease --- are
genuinely ambiguous, and a degree of label noise is unavoidable. This has two
practical consequences for modelling. First, it places a soft ceiling on achievable
Dice that is below $1.0$ even for a perfect model, because the ``ground truth'' itself
carries inter-rater uncertainty. Second, it argues for training procedures that are
robust to noisy labels --- heavy augmentation, ensembling, and avoiding overconfident
fits --- all of which our recipe provides. We treat the provided annotations as the
authoritative target while remaining mindful that the hardest residual errors
(\S\ref{sec:results}) partly reflect this intrinsic ambiguity rather than model
failure alone.

\paragraph{Internal test split.}
The challenge withholds validation-set annotations, so to obtain an honest,
ground-truth-backed performance estimate --- and the sample predictions in this
report --- we deterministically hold out \textbf{10} training cases (seed $1337$,
recorded in \texttt{holdout\_test\_cases.txt}) that are \emph{never} used for model
fitting. The remaining $\sim\!284$ cases are passed to nnU-Net's own five-fold
cross-validation, which additionally yields an out-of-fold (OOF) prediction for every
training case --- the most honest large-sample estimate of generalisation we report.

\label{sec:approach}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the end-to-end pipeline. Each stage is a separate,
independently testable module in our codebase, configured entirely through
environment variables so that the identical code runs on a workstation, a SLURM node,

\subsection{Skull-stripping}
All four modalities are co-registered, so we estimate a single brain mask and apply
it to every modality (zeroing non-brain voxels). We use HD-BET (GPU, batched in a
single model load for throughput) by default and SynthStrip as a contrast- and
age-agnostic alternative. Crucially, the \emph{identical} procedure is applied at
training and inference, so the network never sees a train/test domain gap. Brain
extraction also reduces the effective field of view, letting the network's fixed
patch budget concentrate on brain tissue.

\subsection{Segmentation network}
We adopt nnU-Net~v2 with the \textbf{ResEnc-L} planner in the \texttt{3d\_fullres}
configuration. nnU-Net derives the patch size, target spacing, normalisation and
network topology from the dataset fingerprint; the residual encoder increases
representational capacity while staying within the $96\,$GB of the target GPU.
Section~\ref{sec:arch} details the architecture.

\subsection{Training as a five-fold ensemble}
With only $\sim\!284$ fit-able cases, ensembling is the single most reliable accuracy
lever. We train the standard five-fold cross-validation and average softmax
probabilities at inference, with mirror test-time augmentation. Each fold uses the
nnU-Net default objective and schedule, detailed in \S\ref{sec:impl}.
Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} shows the experimental protocol: the 10-case held-out split
is removed first and never seen by any model; the remaining cases are partitioned into
five folds; each fold trains a model on four partitions and predicts the fifth,
yielding both an out-of-fold prediction for every training case (for honest
large-sample evaluation) and five checkpoints that are softmax-averaged for inference

\subsection{Lesion-wise post-processing}
\label{sec:postproc}
Because the lesion-wise metric punishes small false positives, we perform

outright (preserving tumour-core volume), while tiny ED components are removed.

\section{Network Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
The ResEnc-L network is a 3D encoder--decoder U-Net with residual convolutional
blocks in the encoder, instance normalisation, LeakyReLU activations, strided
convolutions for down-sampling, transposed convolutions for up-sampling, skip
connections at every resolution, and deep supervision on the decoder
(Figure~\ref{fig:unet}). The encoder begins at 32 feature maps and doubles at each
stage up to 320 channels in the bottleneck across (typically) six resolution stages.
The four MRI modalities form the four input channels; the output has five channels
(background + four tumour classes) passed through a softmax. The residual block
(Figure~\ref{fig:resblock}) is the key difference from the vanilla nnU-Net encoder:
a two-convolution residual unit with an identity (or $1\!\times\!1\!\times\!1$
projection) shortcut, which eases optimisation of the deeper, higher-capacity
encoder.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}
\subsection{Hardware}
Training and inference run on a single \textbf{NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000} GPU
(96\,GB VRAM, Blackwell generation) with 8 CPU cores and 56--64\,GB of system RAM,
scheduled through SLURM on the Emory HyPER C3 community cluster. The large VRAM
comfortably hosts the ResEnc-L patch size at full resolution without gradient
checkpointing. The H100/A100 partitions were deliberately avoided as they were
decommissioned at the start of June~2026; the entire project was executed within a
fixed compute budget of a few GPU-days across \emph{all} jobs, which directly
constrained the training schedule (see below).

\subsection{Software / framework}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{nnU-Net v2} (\texttt{nnunetv2}\,$\ge$\,2.5) --- planning, training,
        inference, ensembling.
  \item \textbf{PyTorch 2.x / CUDA 12.8} --- Blackwell-compatible wheels.
  \item \textbf{HD-BET / SynthStrip} --- skull-stripping back-ends.
  \item \textbf{NiBabel, SimpleITK, SciPy, scikit-image} --- medical-image I/O and
        connected-component post-processing.
  \item \textbf{Matplotlib, pandas} --- curve/figure generation and metric tables.
  \item Packaged as an installable \texttt{brats\_ped} package
        (\texttt{pip install -e .}); containerised with Docker (CUDA 12.8 runtime
        base) and an equivalent Apptainer definition for the HPC.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training configuration}
Each fold uses the nnU-Net default recipe, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:train}:
a combined \textbf{Dice + cross-entropy} loss (robust to the class imbalance of
Table~\ref{tab:labelstats}), SGD with Nesterov momentum $0.99$, an initial learning
rate of $10^{-2}$ with polynomial decay (power $0.9$), deep supervision with
resolution-weighted loss, and extensive on-the-fly data augmentation (random
rotation, scaling, elastic-free spatial transforms, Gaussian noise and blur,
brightness and contrast perturbation, simulated low resolution, gamma correction and
axis mirroring). Under our compute budget we used a reduced-epoch trainer
(\texttt{nnUNetTrainer\_500epochs}) per fold so that the full five-fold ensemble fits
the budget while retaining the dominant accuracy gain of ensembling.

\centering
\caption{Training hyper-parameters (per fold).}
\label{tab:train}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Hyper-parameter & Value \\
\midrule

Optimiser          & SGD, Nesterov momentum $0.99$ \\

\subsection{Checkpointing and resumption}
nnU-Net writes \texttt{checkpoint\_best.pth} (best exponential-moving-average
pseudo-Dice) and \texttt{checkpoint\_latest.pth} (every 50 epochs). Our SLURM job is
\texttt{--requeue}-enabled and auto-detects an existing checkpoint to continue
training with \texttt{--c}, so a GPU or time-limit interruption loses at most
$\sim\!50$ epochs of work --- essential for running long jobs within short scheduler
time-slices. To race multiple folds without output-path collisions, each fold is
trained under a distinct results namespace. Training/validation loss and pseudo-Dice
curves are exported automatically by \texttt{plot\_training.py}
(Figure~\ref{fig:curves}).

\subsection{Software engineering and code structure}
The system is organised as an installable Python package (\texttt{brats\_ped}) with a
clean separation of concerns: \texttt{config.py} centralises every path, label, planner
choice and post-processing rule (all environment-overridable); each pipeline stage is
an independently runnable module (\texttt{skull\_strip.py}, \texttt{prepare\_dataset.py},
\texttt{train.py}, \texttt{inference.py}, \texttt{postprocess.py}) exposed both as a CLI
and as a console entry point; evaluation is split into voxel-wise (\texttt{evaluate.py})
and lesion-wise (\texttt{evaluate\_lesionwise.py}) scorers so the official metric and a
fast sanity metric can be computed independently; and \texttt{predict\_entry.py} chains
the full strip$\to$predict$\to$post-process flow as the container entry point. Two
thin drivers wrap these for the two execution modes: \texttt{run\_pipeline.sh} for a
single local GPU and the \texttt{slurm/} sbatch scripts for the cluster. This structure
means each stage can be unit-tested, swapped, or re-run in isolation, and that the
exact same code paths execute locally, on SLURM, and in the submission container ---
the foundation of the reproducibility claims in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Inference and submission packaging}
At inference the five fold checkpoints are loaded, softmax outputs are averaged with
mirror TTA, the argmax label map is post-processed (\S\ref{sec:postproc}) and written
as \texttt{<SubjectID>.nii.gz}. The full chain --- skull-strip $\to$ predict $\to$
post-process --- is wrapped in a Docker/Apptainer container conforming to the
BraTS-Lighthouse algorithm contract (reads \texttt{/input}, writes \texttt{/output}),
so the same artifact used for the held-out evaluation is what is submitted. On the
official 91-case validation set the container produced all 91 label maps for
leaderboard submission.

\subsection{Reproducibility and determinism}
The pipeline is engineered for repeatability. All paths are environment-variable
overridable, so the identical code runs unmodified on a workstation, a SLURM node, and
inside the container. The held-out split is fixed by a constant seed ($1337$) and
recorded to disk, so the train/test partition is byte-for-byte reproducible. The
container pins the CUDA-12.8 base image and installs the project with
\texttt{pip install -e .}, fixing the dependency set. nnU-Net's planning is
deterministic given the dataset fingerprint. Residual non-determinism from cuDNN
kernel selection and floating-point reduction order remains (as for any GPU training),
but the five-fold ensembling and the macroscopic metric stability across folds
(Table~\ref{tab:perfold}) make the reported results robust to it.

stray components cause under the lesion-wise metric.

\subsection{Comparison to the literature}
For reference, the rank-1 BraTS-PED~2025 system~\cite{yi2025} --- a three-model
ensemble (nnU-Net + Swin~UNETR + HFF-Net) --- reported held-out lesion-wise Dice of
$0.918$ (TC) and $0.926$ (WT), with ET ($0.73$) and CC ($0.63$) as the hardest
regions. Our single-backbone five-fold nnU-Net ensemble reaches $0.797$ (TC) and
$0.834$ (WT) in cross-validation under a far smaller compute budget and \emph{without}
a transformer branch or external pre-training; the relative ordering of region
difficulty (ET, CC hardest) matches theirs, confirming the rare classes as the
principal headroom (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Computational cost and efficiency}
The full project ran within a few GPU-days across \emph{all} jobs on a single
RTX~Pro~6000. Skull-stripping the entire cohort with batched HD-BET is a one-off
pre-processing cost of the order of minutes thanks to the single-model-load batching.
nnU-Net planning/pre-processing is likewise a one-off CPU/GPU step. Training is the
dominant cost: each of the five folds runs the 500-epoch schedule independently, and
folds are submitted in parallel across available GPU nodes to compress wall-clock
time. At inference, the five-fold ensemble with mirror TTA processes a case in
roughly a minute on the target GPU, dominated by sliding-window prediction; the
skull-strip and post-processing add only seconds. The container therefore comfortably
fits the per-case time limits of the BraTS-Lighthouse submission environment.
Checkpoint-resumable training (\S\ref{sec:impl}) means the schedule is robust to the
short, pre-emptible time-slices typical of a shared cluster --- no run loses more than
$\sim\!50$ epochs to an interruption.

\subsection{Error analysis}
Three recurring failure modes explain most of the residual error. First,
\emph{rare-class false positives}: small stray ET or CC components in cases where the
class is in fact absent, each scoring zero under the lesion-wise metric --- the exact
target of our post-processing, which recovers part but not all of the loss. Second,
\emph{ET/NET boundary confusion}: the enhancing rim and the non-enhancing core are
visually contiguous and the precise enhancement threshold is subjective, so voxels
near the interface are frequently mislabelled between the two, depressing ET more than
the composite TC (which absorbs the swap). Third, \emph{outlier anatomy}: a small
number of cases with atypical tumour location or acquisition drive the long tails of
the per-region distributions (the large standard deviations in Table~\ref{tab:cv}),
pulling the mean well below the median. These observations motivate the
uncertainty-aware and two-stage extensions in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

% =============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our results reaffirm the central lesson of the recent BraTS cluster: a
well-engineered nnU-Net pipeline, with the \emph{right} data handling, beats
architectural novelty for tumour segmentation. Three design choices matter most for
the pediatric setting. First, \textbf{skull-stripping} removes a large, high-contrast
confounder that is otherwise irrelevant to the tumour, and applying it identically at
train and test time eliminates a domain gap that would otherwise inflate test error.
Second, \textbf{ensembling} is disproportionately valuable given the small cohort:
averaging five folds smooths the per-fold variance that a single model trained on
$\sim\!227$ cases would exhibit. Third, \textbf{lesion-wise post-processing} converts
volumetric accuracy into leaderboard points by suppressing the small false positives
that the metric punishes; the enhancing tumour and cystic component, present in only
$67\%$ and $35\%$ of cases respectively, benefit most.

The gap between our cross-validation TC/WT ($0.80/0.83$) and the rank-1 2025 system
($0.92/0.93$) is consistent with the differences in method scope: the leading system
ensembles three architecturally diverse models with external BraTS-2021 pre-training
and a two-stage strip-then-finetune schedule, whereas ours is a single-backbone,
budget-constrained five-fold ensemble. The strong agreement between our
cross-validation and held-out estimates on the well-populated regions
(Figure~\ref{fig:bars}) gives us confidence that the cross-validation numbers are not
optimistic, and that the headroom lies overwhelmingly in the rare classes.

\paragraph{Interpreting voxel-wise versus lesion-wise scores.}
The two metric families tell complementary stories and should not be conflated.
Voxel-wise Dice rewards getting the \emph{bulk} of a region right and is dominated by
the large structures, which is why our held-out voxel-wise WT/TC ($0.91/0.88$) look
strong. The lesion-wise metric instead asks whether \emph{each} lesion --- however
small --- is found and not accompanied by spurious extras, which is harder and yields
the lower, more conservative composite numbers ($0.83/0.80$). The challenge ranks on
the lesion-wise variant precisely because, clinically, missing a small second focus or
inventing a phantom lesion matters as much as mis-sizing the main mass. Our design
choices (ensembling for detection sensitivity, post-processing for false-positive
suppression) target the lesion-wise metric directly, and the gap between the two metric
families on our results quantifies how much of the error is small-component detection
rather than gross volumetric mis-segmentation.

\paragraph{Clinical implications.}
For the clinical workflow that motivates this challenge, the well-segmented composite
regions are the most immediately useful: whole-tumour and tumour-core volumes
($0.83$/$0.80$ lesion-wise Dice) support reproducible volumetry for RAPNO-style
response assessment and radiotherapy target definition, removing a major source of
inter-rater variability and a substantial manual-contouring burden. The weaker
rare-class performance (ET, CC) means the tool is best positioned as an
expert-in-the-loop aid --- producing an editable first-pass segmentation a
radiologist refines --- rather than a fully autonomous system, especially for the
enhancement-based sub-regions that inform tumour grading. The deterministic,
containerised packaging is itself clinically relevant: identical, auditable behaviour
across sites is a prerequisite for any prospective deployment or multi-centre study.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Several limitations temper the results and point to concrete next steps.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Rare-class variance.} ET and CC scores are dominated by a small
        number of outlier cases that skew the mean far below the median; the
        lesion-wise metric's all-or-nothing treatment of empty regions amplifies
        this. The 10-case held-out ED score ($0.0$ voxel-wise) is not
        representative --- the cross-validation ED estimate ($0.792$ LWDSC) should be
        preferred. A larger held-out split would tighten these estimates.
  \item \textbf{Compute budget.} A fixed few-GPU-day budget forced a reduced
        (500-epoch) schedule and precluded the longer training, larger ResEnc-XL
        encoder, and multi-architecture ensembling used by the top 2025 teams.
  \item \textbf{Single backbone, single-stage.} We use one architecture family and a
        single training stage; the rank-1/rank-2 systems show that architectural
        diversity and a strip-then-finetune schedule add several Dice points.
  \item \textbf{Post-processing thresholds} are fixed (literature- and
        statistics-derived) rather than learned or cross-validated per class, so they
        are likely sub-optimal.
  \item \textbf{No uncertainty estimates.} The pipeline emits hard label maps only;
        clinical use would benefit from calibrated per-voxel uncertainty to flag
        ambiguous cases for human review.
\end{itemize}
Promising extensions include: (i)~a rank-2-style \emph{two-stage} schedule
(pre-train on skull-stripped data, fine-tune on the original data); (ii)~adding a
Swin~UNETR or frequency-domain branch to the ensemble as in rank-1; (iii)~per-class
cross-validated post-processing thresholds; and (iv)~uncertainty-aware losses and
test-time augmentation variance to surface outlier cases.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a reproducible, containerised pipeline for pediatric brain-tumour
segmentation that pairs consistent skull-stripping with a residual-encoder nnU-Net
five-fold ensemble and lesion-wise post-processing. The approach is grounded in the
repeatedly validated recipe of recent BraTS winners, is engineered for robust,
checkpoint-resumable training on commodity-scheduled GPUs within a tight compute
budget, and is delivered as a BraTS-Lighthouse-compliant Docker/Apptainer container.
In cross-validation it attains lesion-wise Dice of $0.797$ (TC) and $0.834$ (WT),
with the rare enhancing-tumour and cystic-component classes identified as the primary
headroom. We release all code, training/inference scripts, the container definition,
and this report to support reproducibility and further pediatric neuro-oncology
research.

% =============================================================================
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
The error analysis (\S\ref{sec:results}) and limitations (\S\ref{sec:limits})
suggest a concrete, prioritised roadmap, ordered by expected return per unit of
additional compute:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Two-stage strip-then-finetune.} Adopt the rank-2 schedule: pre-train
        on skull-stripped data, then fine-tune on the original (non-stripped) volumes.
        This adds robustness to the skull-stripping itself becoming a single point of
        failure, and was a clear differentiator in 2025.
  \item \textbf{Architectural diversity in the ensemble.} Add a BraTS-2021-pretrained
        Swin~UNETR and/or a frequency-domain branch, as in the rank-1 system, and
        ensemble at the probability level. Diverse errors cancel; this is the most
        reliable accuracy lever beyond a single backbone.
  \item \textbf{Longer schedule / larger encoder.} Lift the budget-imposed 500-epoch
        cap toward the 1000-epoch default and evaluate the ResEnc-XL planner, which
        the target 96\,GB GPU can host.
  \item \textbf{Learned post-processing thresholds.} Cross-validate the per-class
        component-size thresholds (Table~\ref{tab:pp}) rather than fixing them, and
        consider a small classifier to gate rare-class predictions per case.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty and outlier flagging.} Use TTA/fold variance or an
        explicit uncertainty head to surface the long-tail outlier cases for human
        review --- directly addressing the dominant source of rare-class error.
\end{enumerate}

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion (Summary)}
In summary, this work delivers a complete, reproducible BraTS-PED 2026 Task-2 system:
a skull-stripping front-end, a five-fold residual-encoder nnU-Net ensemble, and
lesion-wise post-processing, packaged as a portable container and validated against
ground truth at two evaluation scales. It reaches lesion-wise Dice of $0.797$ (TC)
and $0.834$ (WT) in cross-validation and voxel-wise Dice of $0.876$/$0.914$ (TC/WT)
on a held-out internal test set, with a clearly characterised path to closing the gap
to the leading 2025 systems. All artifacts are released to support reproducibility.

\section{Command Reference}
\label{app:cmd}
The complete pipeline is driven by a handful of commands (see the repository
\texttt{README.md} for full detail).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Environment:} \texttt{bash code/setup\_env.sh} --- creates
        \texttt{venv/}, installs the CUDA-12.8 PyTorch wheel and the \texttt{brats\_ped}
        package and all dependencies.
  \item \textbf{Skull-strip:} \texttt{python code/skull\_strip.py --split both
        --method auto --device gpu}.
  \item \textbf{Prepare + plan:} \texttt{python code/prepare\_dataset.py};
        \texttt{python code/train.py --step preprocess}.
  \item \textbf{Train (SLURM):} \texttt{bash code/slurm/submit\_train.sh 0 1 2 3 4}
        --- resource-aware submission, chained \texttt{prep$\to$train}, resumable.
  \item \textbf{Infer:} \texttt{python code/inference.py --input data\_ss/validation
        --output predictions/raw --raw-input --folds 0 1 2 3 4 --tta}.
  \item \textbf{Post-process:} \texttt{python code/postprocess.py --input
        predictions/raw --output predictions/postprocessed}.
  \item \textbf{Evaluate:} \texttt{python code/evaluate\_lesionwise.py --pred
        predictions/postprocessed --gt <labelsTr> --output results/lesionwise.csv}.
  \item \textbf{Container:} \texttt{bash docker/build.sh} (stage model + build);
        \texttt{bash docker/run\_sample.sh} (run + score on held-out cases).
\end{itemize}

\section{Output Format}
\label{app:fmt}
The container reads \texttt{/input/<SubjectID>/} containing the four modalities
(\texttt{<SubjectID>-\{t1n,t1c,t2w,t2f\}.nii.gz}) and writes a single integer-valued
label map \texttt{/output/<SubjectID>.nii.gz} with voxel values
$\{0,1,2,3,4\}=\{$background, ET, NET, CC, ED$\}$, in the input geometry. This matches
the BraTS-Lighthouse algorithm contract, so the same artifact serves the held-out
evaluation and the official leaderboard submission.
